Start Here: What GEO Research Has Actually Shown

Start Here: What GEO Research Has Actually Shown

More and more people ask AI directly what to buy and whom to turn to. Before AI answers, it first decides whether to go online and look things up. Then it rewrites the question into its own search queries, pulls back a batch of material, and picks a few pieces to write into an answer. Readers often leave as soon as they have read it. Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) aims to get AI to mention, cite and recommend you during this process. There is plenty of GEO advice out there, and very little of it is reliable. Many impressive numbers come from simplified experiments, many "success stories" are a single screenshot, and many practices rest on nothing more than "everyone says so". This book separates what research has proven, what people in the field believe, and what no one knows yet, and for every point it tells you how strong the evidence is.

What research is fairly sure of

  1. AI answers keep most readers on the platform. Large-scale experiments with real users have shown that AI answers really are taking traffic away: only 5.2% of ChatGPT conversations click through to an outside website, compared with 31.1% for Google Search. In one randomized experiment, the group made to use Google AI Mode clicked through to outside websites 18.8 percentage points less often. This is the conclusion with the strongest evidence in the whole book. (Chapter 10)
  2. Being cited rarely brings clicks. Researchers observed this repeatedly on real AI search: when an AI summary appeared, the share of people clicking traditional search results fell from 15% to 8%, and only about 1% clicked a source cited in the AI summary. This is observational data, so it shows only that the two things occur together. (Chapters 10 and 14)
  3. AI often does not search at all. Researchers tested this repeatedly on real AI search: given the same 1,000 questions, one model searched only 14% of the time, and another 83% of the time. Queries phrased as questions, and long queries, are the most likely to trigger AI answers, and three studies point the same way. (Chapter 2)
  4. There is no single "AI ranking". Researchers tested this repeatedly on real AI search: for the same question, the URLs cited by ChatGPT, Copilot, Google and Perplexity had an average overlap score of only 0.0079 (1 means identical), and in 84.9% of cases, any two of them shared not a single URL. (Chapters 4 and 12)
  5. The same AI answers differently every time, too. Researchers tested this repeatedly on real AI search, measuring every day for 45 days in a row: about two-thirds of the sources changed every day, and asking the same question 10 times on the same day looked much the same. If you appear 4 times out of 10, you can be 95% confident that your true mention rate lies somewhere between about 12% and 74%. (Chapter 9)
  6. AI answers are mostly built on other people's websites. Researchers tested this repeatedly on real AI search, and several teams working in different languages and on different AI platforms got results pointing the same way: brands' own websites make up only about 3% to 17% of AI citations. This is a descriptive measurement. It tells you where the battlefield is; it does not promise that you will win once you get there. (Chapter 5)
  7. AI knowing you does not mean AI will recommend you. This comes from researchers' repeated testing on real AI search, plus a large amount of testing by people in the industry themselves. In one set of tests, when asked about a new product by name, AI recognised it 99.4% of the time; when asked to recommend products in that category, it mentioned the product only 3.32% of the time. (Chapters 5 and 13)
  8. Being listed as a source does not mean your content was used. Researchers tested this repeatedly on real Chinese-language AI search platforms: 39.3% of listed sources were "silent citations", and only 8.3% of the brands that appeared in citations actually made it into the answer. Going the other way, 14% to 53% of the statements in answers drew on search results without marking a citation. (Chapter 7)
  9. The language of the question is a market gate. Researchers tested this repeatedly on real AI search: in Google AI Overviews on health topics, asking in the local native language raised the share of citations from local sources by 3.5 to 13.5 times. If you measure only in English, you may not see your local competitors at all. (Chapter 4)
  10. A "sponsored" label does not stop AI from selling. Large-scale experiments with real users have shown this: on a purpose-built e-book shopping platform, when AI was told to promote products, users chose the sponsored product 61.2% of the time. With a "sponsored" label and an advance warning to users, the figure was still 55.5%, and when AI promoted products subtly, only 9.5% of users noticed. (Chapter 11)

Common claims the evidence does not support

"Add statistics, quotations and cited sources, and visibility rises by 40%." That number is a relative gain measured under narrow conditions: a fixed set of 5 candidate documents, answers written by GPT-3.5, and share of words as the yardstick. It does not mean you become easier to find, and it does not mean more traffic. Later studies retested it under three kinds of conditions, with different results. Under test conditions with given web pages, the material was handed directly to ten newer models, and the three tactics went from +42.6, +32.8 and +27.7 percentage points to -0.33, -0.28 and -0.79. Also with given pages, but with real AI search reading pages the researchers had published, rewrites that added statistics still beat plain originals, yet every one of those numbers was made up and AI did not check them. Closest to real life, researchers tested repeatedly on real AI search and let it find pages on its own; there this kind of writing showed no effect, and whether the page had been indexed mattered more. (Chapters 0 and 8)

"If I rank well on Google, AI will naturally recommend me." Researchers tested this repeatedly on real AI search: the overlap between the sites AI cited and Google's top 10 was 4.0% for GPT-4o, 11.1% for Gemini, 12.6% for Claude and 15.2% for Perplexity. Search ranking is the foundation; it does not get you through the gate on its own. (Chapters 1 and 4)

"AI traffic grew 5.7 times, which proves the optimization worked." Pages on the same website that nobody had touched also grew to 3.5 times over the same period. After subtracting the platform's own growth, the optimized pages came to about 1.82 times, and because the method checks were not clean enough, this counts only as suggestive evidence. Over the same period, the website's organic clicks from Google actually fell by about 25%. (Chapters 10 and 14)

"Get to the top of the list AI reads, and you win." In a controllable experimental system, the raw observational data showed a citation gap of 42.3 percentage points between position 1 and position 5. After controlling for other factors, only 7.9 percentage points remained, and when checked again on a batch of samples held back in advance, the gap was 0. Sources near the top are often more relevant to begin with. (Chapter 6)

"Put up an llms.txt, or prepare a separate Markdown version for AI, and you will be cited more." There is currently no evidence that llms.txt works on its own, and several independent server logs show that the major AI crawlers almost never request it. Most observations also show that live fetching, when AI is answering a question, reads only the HTML. (Chapter 3)

"Hide an instruction for AI in your web page, and you can make it recommend you." Under given-page test conditions, hidden instructions had an average success rate of only 0.3%. Some AIs even penalise the brand behind the injection. The evidence on this is weak, but the risk is real. (Chapters 5 and 11)

"AI loves citing community forums, so post there and you will be recommended." The share of citations going to communities varies widely between systems, from 0.1% and 8.5% up to 13.4%. The claim that "brand participation in communities raises AI visibility" is currently supported only by before-and-after comparisons from people in the industry; no randomized or quasi-experimental study has proven it yet. (Chapter 15)

"Blocking AI crawlers means opting out of AI answers; not blocking them means AI can definitely read you." AI crawlers come in three types: those that collect training data, those that build an index, and those that fetch pages live. Blocking the crawlers that collect training data will not make you disappear from real-time answers. Large-scale real-user data shows that sites that block them do not get less referral traffic from ChatGPT on average, though this is only a correlation. Going the other way, allowing access in robots.txt does not mean AI can read you. Many people in the industry believe AI crawlers are often blocked at the website's firewall, and content that exists only after JavaScript assembles the page does not exist at all for most AI crawlers. (Chapter 3)

What is still unsettled

Several of the questions people care about most have no answer yet. First, whether GEO can reliably bring traffic or business: no study has proven it, and the only study that seriously separated the effects found, statistically, only "some signs". Second, whether being mentioned by others has an effect of its own: one side's measurements say yes, another side's research says the effect disappears once you account for how readable the website is, and the two may be acting at different stages. Third, "get third parties to write about you and recommendations will rise": so far there is only observational and before-and-after evidence, with no validation from controlled experiments. Fourth, whether visitors from AI are more likely to buy: several industry reports say yes, other reports say they hardly buy at all, and for now there is no telling which is right. Fifth, how many times you need to ask each question: people argue for anything from 3 to more than 20, and the only answer known to be wrong is "once". When you face these questions, the most reliable evidence is data you measure yourself: fixed questions, asked many times, with a control group kept aside. (Chapters 5, 10, 13 and 16)

How to read this book

The book follows the seven gates AI passes through when it answers a question: whether to search, can it get in the door, can it find you, does it pick you, will it mention you, does it really use you, and will people come over. Chapter 0 teaches you how to read evidence, Chapter 1 draws the map of the seven gates, Chapters 2 to 7 take the gates one at a time, Chapters 8 to 12 deal with topics that span several gates, Chapters 13 to 15 are case studies, and Chapter 16 gathers the questions still under debate. The book comes in two editions. The plain-language edition explains the reasoning in everyday words, while the research edition keeps the evidence tables and research details behind every conclusion, so you can check any chapter against its counterpart. The studies this book draws on range from late 2023 to October 2026, and AI search changes fast, so the book marks its version and will keep being updated.
